Freedom Forever: everything around a solar install

At Freedom Forever, a residential solar installer operating across the United States, I designed both sides of an install: the field app the crews work from, the customer app homeowners use to follow their project, and the internal platform operators live in all day, including Lightspeed, the project pipeline, and an AI customer-support agent.

Role
Product designer
Timeline
2021 — 2026
Platform
iOS & Android, desktop web
Tools
Figma, Claude Code, Claude Agent SDK, ElevenLabs, Mixpanel

01 · Context

A residential solar install moves through dozens of milestones over weeks — site survey, permitting, install, inspection, activation — and it involves field crews, project managers, and an anxious homeowner who just signed a 25-year commitment. The tools holding that together were built for the office; the work happens on driveways and rooftops.

A fast-growing national installer runs on back-office throughput: pricing, permits, commissions, scheduling, supplier coordination. That work was spread across spreadsheets, legacy tools, and a growing internal platform — and operators were context-switching between five systems to finish one task.

02 · The field app

The field app is the crew's window into the job: schedules, site details, documents, and status updates from a phone, often with gloves on and sun on the screen. I designed it for one-handed use and fast scanning — the measure of success was how little time a tech had to spend in it.

Instrumentation mattered as much as the interface: we used Mixpanel to see where crews actually got stuck, and let that reshape the flows.

03 · The customer app

The customer app gives homeowners a clear view of a long, opaque process — where their project stands, what happens next, and who to talk to. Fewer surprises for the homeowner means fewer support calls for the operation.

This one I both designed and coded, working in Figma and Claude Code side by side. Designing in the production medium collapsed the usual handoff cycle: what I mocked in the morning could be running on a device by the afternoon.

The fastest design review is a build on your phone.

04 · Internal tools

  • Lightspeed — the solar project pipeline management system, where a project's whole life from sale to activation is tracked and moved.
  • Scheduling — matching crews, trucks, and jobs across regions, where a bad screen means a wasted truck roll.
  • Commissions — the tooling that calculates and explains pay for the sales organization, where trust depends on legibility.
  • AI customer support — a support agent and the agent framework behind it, built with the Claude Agent SDK, with ElevenLabs for voice. This is where my design work and AI-builder work merged.

05 · Designing for the ten-thousandth use

Operators spend eight hours a day in these tools. That changes what good design means: density becomes kindness, consistency becomes speed, and every extra click is multiplied by hundreds of people, every day. I designed from first principles about the work itself — watching how operators actually moved through a task, then making the software match.

Consumer software gets judged in the first five minutes. Operator software gets judged in the second month.

06 · What carried forward

Designing both sides of the same operation taught me that the field app and the customer app are really one product — every status a crew logs is a moment of reassurance (or anxiety) for a homeowner. Designing the pair together, on a shared component language, is what made each one simpler.

This is where I learned to treat AI agents as a design material. A support agent isn't a chatbot bolted onto a help page — it's a coworker that needs the same things operators do: context, guardrails, and a clear picture of the pipeline. Designing the framework around the agent turned out to matter more than designing its chat window.
